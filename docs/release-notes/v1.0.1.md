Fixes a layout bug on narrow phones, gives the crop screen more room, and
improves the quality of the exported photo.

## Fixed

**Paper size buttons ran off the edge of the screen.** On a phone 411 pixels
wide — a common size — the `Letter` button overflowed its row by 21 pixels and
was clipped. The `A4` and `Letter` buttons also used the same icon, which
distinguished nothing while taking the width that caused the problem, so the
icons are gone and the labels now shrink to fit.

## Improved

**The crop screen gives the photo more room.** About 120 device pixels were
reclaimed from the controls: `Rotate` now shares a row with the `Photo Format`
heading instead of taking a row of its own, the frame sits closer to the edges
of the screen, and the photo switcher strip is more compact. This is the screen
where a face is judged against a guide, so the photo should be as large as the
phone allows.

**Better colour in the printed photo.** Exports previously used the usual JPEG
4:2:0 chroma subsampling, which keeps full brightness detail but only a quarter
of the colour detail. Faces are mostly smooth colour, which is exactly where
that shows at print size. Exports now use full 4:4:4 colour at quality 95.

**Correct filtering when enlarging.** Resizing always used box averaging. That
is the right choice when reducing a camera photo to print size — it reads every
source pixel — but it blocks up when enlarging, which happens with an
already-compressed photo zoomed in a long way. Enlarging now uses cubic
interpolation.

**Print resolution raised from 300 to 450 DPI.** Each photo is now 620 × 797
pixels instead of 413 × 531. A sheet grows from about 200 kB to about 500 kB and
takes no longer to produce, because the time goes on decoding the camera photo
rather than resizing it.

You are unlikely to see a difference on a normal print: 300 DPI was already at
the limit of what the eye resolves at reading distance and what a consumer photo
printer puts on paper. The extra resolution is headroom for a sheet that is
later enlarged, scanned, or resampled by a professional lab.

## On print quality generally

A photo taken with the app loses nothing meaningful. A 12-megapixel phone held
upright provides around 2177 DPI of detail at normal framing — several times
what print can use — so the export discards detail rather than inventing it.

Quality only suffers with an already-compressed source, such as a photo received
through a messaging app, zoomed in a long way. If a photo looks soft on paper,
the fix is to use the original rather than a shared copy.
